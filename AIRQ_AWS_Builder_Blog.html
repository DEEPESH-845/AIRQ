Build notes

AIRQ: a smog strategy game built on AWS

8 minute read

AIRQ's map once left half of India uncoloured. An effect meant to mark threatened districts was forcing MapLibre to lay out its district source again on every frame.

Understanding a district's air-quality forecast means looking beyond its AQI number. The wind matters, as do possible sources upstream and the share of pollution an intervention could reach. AIRQ makes that investigation playable. You choose an Indian district, inspect its 48-hour forecast, trace the incoming air and spend a small game budget on interventions. Outside the game, you can submit green actions for rewards or register a field for satellite fire checks.

The project covers 790 districts in its dataset. Their air-quality values come from a model, so the map labels them as estimates. There isn't a monitoring station behind every coloured polygon.

Playing with tomorrow's forecast

Select a district and its panel shows the current air-quality estimate, the forecast and an estimated breakdown of pollution sources. Wind particles change colour as they cross districts. “Trace the smoke” adds a 36-hour back-trajectory, with fire clusters near the path.

The trace gives you somewhere to look upstream. It cannot establish that a particular field caused the pollution in your district; the transport calculation is a simplified approximation.

In the Defend game, you have four points to spend. Crop-residue management, dust control and industrial fuel changes affect different source shares. Your choices produce a scenario overlaid on tomorrow's forecast.

The smog tower is a revealing option. It costs half the daily budget and, under the game's assumptions, cuts the vehicle and dust components by only 0.4%. The district-wide result barely changes. You can see that before committing the rest of your points.

These effects are game assumptions, not validated estimates of real policy outcomes. The useful comparison is between the interventions within the scenario: how much of the estimated pollution each reaches, and how much of your budget it uses.

Building world.json

Most of the map's data arrives in one file. A Python pipeline combines Copernicus CAMS particulate-matter forecasts from the Open-Meteo Air Quality API, Open-Meteo wind and boundary-layer data, and NASA FIRMS VIIRS fire detections. Its output is world.json.

For each district, the pipeline calculates a PM-based AQI estimate using 24-hour rolling PM2.5 and PM10 values and CPCB breakpoint tables. It takes the higher of the two subindices. This covers particulate matter; it doesn't reproduce the full official AQI across all pollutants. The forecast itself comes from CAMS. AIRQ doesn't train a separate forecasting model.

The source breakdown needs more assumptions. It starts with literature-based regional priors, then adjusts them for factors including nearby coal capacity, overnight mixing and fires along the trajectory. The interface marks these shares as estimates with low or medium confidence.

EventBridge Scheduler starts a Standard Step Functions workflow every four hours. The tick Lambda builds the file, writes it to private S3 storage and archives a copy. Later steps check registered fields, send nearby-fire alerts and publish smog-raid events. EventBridge and SNS carry those raid events to the push Lambda.

The frontend is React and TypeScript, built with Vite. MapLibre GL draws the map, and a custom canvas layer draws the wind particles. CloudFront serves the app and the world snapshot. Opening another district uses that shared snapshot, without fetching the external feeds again.

Questions and reward claims go through API Gateway to separate Lambdas. DynamoDB stores player state, proof records, subscriptions and question quotas. AWS SAM defines the deployment.

Getting the refresh to finish

A refresh requests air-quality data for 790 district centroids and wind data for a 546-point grid. That's 1,336 requested locations, before the fire feeds.

The external requests are batched, with a 12-second pause between batches. A rate-limit response adds a 35-second wait before retrying. Once retries enter the picture, orchestration has to accommodate several minutes of waiting. The deployment uses a Standard Step Functions workflow; Express Workflows have a five-minute limit.

Each feed also has a last-good cached copy. If a fetch fails, the pipeline can use that copy and mark it stale. The top bar says “Some feeds delayed.” The map stays available, and the player can see that part of the data is old.

Failures in the follow-up field and alert jobs are caught separately, so they don't discard a completed world refresh.

Fixing the district animation

The threatened-district effect combines a breathing haze with a marching dashed border. The first implementation animated a data-driven paint property. MapLibre kept laying out the district source again, and half the country remained uncoloured.

The fix was to animate constant paint values only. The dashed border needed another workaround: MapLibre couldn't animate its dash offset, so AIRQ prepares 14 line layers with different dash phases and shows one at a time.

The repository includes a UI regression script for the zoom-out colouring bug. It also checks browser Back and Escape behaviour. Other small fixes address how people reach the map: touch devices don't get a sticky hover tooltip, reduced-motion preferences affect the animations, and a district link opens its destination without showing the introductory tour first.

The General

AIRQ's advisor, the General, runs on the Strands Agents SDK and Amazon Nova Micro through Bedrock. It has three data tools: look up a district, rank districts and find nearby fires. Districts used in an answer are highlighted on the map.

The prompt asks the model to use tools for factual answers and to describe source shares as estimates. Health guidance takes a separate path. A fixed matrix, informed by CPCB statements and GRAP, is shared by the frontend and Lambda. The model doesn't receive the health-guidance tool. Application code appends the relevant guidance when needed, including in morning briefings.

An output check also blocks certain medical terms and dosage patterns. That filter has limits; it isn't a complete safety guarantee. The fixed guidance can be reviewed independently of whatever wording the model produces.

The advisor initially used Claude Sonnet 4.5. The commit history records a switch to Nova Micro for cost and first-party model access, along with output-tag stripping. Morning briefings are cached by district and four-hour tick. Chat has a quota of ten questions per hashed IP per day.

The deployment can configure a $15 monthly budget alert. It's an alert threshold, not a measured operating cost or a spending cap.

Checking a reward claim

Playing the browser games earns XP. Spendable credits require server-side checks, including daily limits.

For photo claims, approval starts before the model call. The Lambda decodes and re-encodes the upload, removes metadata, and compares exact and perceptual hashes against previous approvals. Perceptual matching can catch some copies that have been resized or saved again.

Higher-value actions need a handwritten, one-time code in the frame. The code lasts 15 minutes. Amazon Nova Lite assesses the visible evidence through Bedrock, and its result is checked alongside the applicable code requirement.

There's a second problem after the photo passes: two requests can arrive together. Both might clear an early limit check before either updates the player's balance. AIRQ uses a conditional write to claim the photo hash, then checks caps again inside a versioned player update. The payout path has to handle that race too.

A photograph still leaves gaps. A bus photo can't prove that someone avoided a car journey. Nova Lite can also accept the wrong image or reject a legitimate one. A working review interface is still needed, along with measured false-accept and false-reject rates.

Satellite Fire Watch

Registered fields use satellite checks instead of photo submissions. Under the current rules, a daily check without a VIIRS fire detection can earn credits. A detected fire starts a cooldown. A long gap in checks resets the run without paying for the unobserved interval.

That result has to remain “no fire detected.” NASA describes how fires can be missed, including fires between observations, under cloud cover, or too small or cool to detect. The feature can't certify that a field had no burning.

Village Fire Pacts let players participate as a group. Weekly settlement waits until Tuesday to allow late satellite passes to count, and conditional writes prevent duplicate settlement payments.

The public pact layer shows a ring only when at least three fields are involved. Its centre is rounded to roughly ten-kilometre precision; individual field pins stay out of that layer. Hindi and Punjabi translations cover the action, field and team screens. Health text remains in English pending human-checked translation.

Some parts of the reward system are still unfinished. Physical reward fulfilment is being arranged, and the photo-review interface needs to be built. DigiLocker, e-RUPI, CSR funding and ground-station correction remain future ideas. Larger participation would also require changes to the current player scans and field jobs.

The next test should put AIRQ in front of people who haven't read its code. The forecast and Defend scenario sit close together, so a useful first check is whether players can tell them apart. Watching someone submit a legitimate action would also reveal friction that the calculation and reward checks can't catch.

Try AIRQ by choosing a district and opening “Trace the smoke.” Then try a Defend plan and compare its effect on tomorrow's forecast. The repository contains the data pipeline, SAM deployment, advisor and reward logic. If a label or interaction is confusing, that specific example would be useful feedback.
